by: frau
NOT ONE POST IN THIRTY SQUARE MILES
FRAU WANDERLUST ABROAD: THE GREAT SAND DUNES

Guten Abend. This week I am leaving the valley floor to make a proper expedition, because a lady can only walk the same fence lines so many nights before the posts begin to look at her. I take my snacks, the raisins and one pretzel, and I go north, toward the mountains. Or I think north. Which way is Germany from here, ja?

And so I arrive at the Great Sand Dunes. Ach. You must understand, these are the tallest dunes in all of North America. The biggest, the Star Dune, rises about 750 feet from its base to its top. I climb only a small one, and still I am breathing like a tea kettle. The main dunefield is about thirty square miles of sand, all piled against the Sangre de Cristos like laundry nobody folded.

A ranger sign explains it, and I read it twice because I lost my place. The sand comes from old lakes and streams in the valley, the Rio Grande too. The southwest wind blows it all toward the low passes in the mountains, and then the storm winds blow the other way and push the ridges back. Back and forth, back and forth, for thousands of years. This I understand. This is also how I walk.

Now, my review of the posts:

- Posts on the dunes: zero.
- Posts on the next dune: also zero.
- Posts in thirty square miles: I counted to eleven and then I forgot what I was counting.
- Straightness of the posts I did not find: excellent, natürlich, because they were imaginary.

I ask myself, how does a place become a national monument with no fence? Herbert Hoover made it a monument in 1932, and in 2004 they made it a national park and preserve, four times bigger. More than ninety years, and nobody puts up one nice post. I find this very trusting of the Americans.

There is also a creek, Medano Creek, where in spring the water comes down in little waves over the sand and the children splash. In November there is no splashing. Only me, and the cold, and a small creature at the edge of the sand who looked at me like I owed it money. I think it was the Chupa. I asked if it had seen the post. It went behind a bush, which is an answer, ja?

On the walk back I met a nice talkative man at a pullout who told me for one hour about the county, the drifters and the trees, who are all going to die, he says. I asked, Herr, do you know where a lady finds some nice pot, something mellow, for the walking? He looked at the mountains and talked about the container. Such a good listener, this valley.

Next week perhaps I go somewhere with fences. And Tuesday the big moon comes, so I will see my shadow again, and maybe it will finally show me the way.
