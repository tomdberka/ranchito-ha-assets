PRAIRIE BOB REVIEWS THE BLOODLESS VALLEY
THE OPINION OF PRAIRIE BOB: ONE STAR, THEY ARE ROCKS

Prairie Bob here. I sell pot. Also I review YouTube videos now, on account of my nephew says that's where the money is. Today's review: "The Bloodless Valley," a half-hour documentary about the Baca Grande up by Crestone, from back when everybody had a tape deck. I watched the whole thing twice. Once ironically, and once because I fell asleep during the barley.

The video opens by telling me that in 1978 the 400-square-mile Baca Grande estate was acquired by Maurice and Hanne Strong, who started a foundation to keep it a home for "the wisdom traditions of the world." Then it says the Navajo called it the Bloodless Valley because nobody ever fought there. I have it on good authority that's because nobody could find it. I have been driving to Crestone for thirty years and I still turn at Moffat and pray.

[photo: ev-20261126-bloodless-valley-stone-seat.jpg | A man sits on what the video calls an ancient healing seat. Prairie Bob has examined the footage frame by frame. It is a rock.]

Chapter Three is called "Ancient Stone Artifacts," and this is where the whole operation falls apart. A fella in a sheepskin vest and sunglasses sits on a rock under a scrubby tree and explains that the stone seats are "a collaborative enterprise between the stones and the ancient peoples." Boys. I have sat on rocks my whole life. That is not a collaborative enterprise. That is a rock.

Another fella in the video says you can't walk more than fifty feet up there without bumping into one. That's not ancient. That's Colorado. He says each seat has its own healing capacity, and this particular one works on the "hara." So I sat on a rock out by the container for forty-five minutes to test it. Results: cold behind, one ant, and the trees are gonna die. Same as before I sat down.

Things in this video Prairie Bob has determined are rocks:
- The healing stone seats. Rocks.
- The stone circle an environmental artist built. Rocks, but in a circle.
- The "eye of the land," where they put a stupa. Couldn't see a rock there. There's one under it, guaranteed.
- The mountain. Big rock.

Then the video really gets going. Tibetan medicine, a Zen center, a hermitage some Carmelite sisters built in 1982, Hopi farming ideas, a seed bank, and test plots of Tibetan barley. It says Crestone was first a gold mining town in the late 1800s. So they came for gold, and now they come for vibes, and as far as I can tell neither one of them pays property tax on time. Costilla County is coming to haul away the container and nobody's hauling away a single rock up there. Think about that.

One lady in the video says contemplation is "a long loving look at the real." That's what I give a fella right before I sell him pot.

Now, I will say this for the north end: business is good. Them folks buy extra. They call it a sacrament. I call it Tuesday. I sell pot. My problem is the drive. It's Highway 17 forever, then County Road T, then a bunch of fellas in robes waving at you like they know something. I used to have a girlfriend up there. Real nice lady. Waaay too hippy-dippy. She wanted me to sit on a healing rock and I said, honey, I brought a camp chair. She said the camp chair had bad energy. Turns out she was right, it was the one with the busted leg, but that is not the point.

Speaking of drifters. That fella in the green hoodie from last night's paper, the one my nephew keeps insisting is Aaron Rodgers, came back by this morning, Thanksgiving Day, asking which way Crestone was. I said north, turn at Moffat, pray. He asked if they had anything up there "for the soul." I offered him pot again. He took a slice of pie instead and stared at the container a long time. The drifters will steal the whole container, boys, and this one's got a strong arm.

Final rating: one star. Two for the mountain shots. Position of Prairie Bob: they are rocks. Bring your own chair. Lock it down, boys.

BOARD DEMANDS TEN-FOOT TURKEY SETBACK
The Board has reviewed a report from WDSU, a New Orleans television station, titled "The anatomy of a deep fried turkey: What makes it explode?" The Board did not need a television station for this. The Board has suspected the turkey for years.

Per the report, deep-fried turkeys were popularized in the 1980s out of Cajun Louisiana, and about 7% of people now fry one for Thanksgiving, according to the National Turkey Federation. The National Fire Protection Association, which discourages the practice, estimates 1,139 home cooking fires on Thanksgiving in 2024. It is the peak day for them every year.

The report advises frying outdoors, ten feet from structures, wooden decks and patios. The Board wishes it entered into the record that this is a setback. A setback is zoning. Costilla County is zoned.

The Board observed the following from the porch today:
- The unpermitted second story, well within ten feet of the container. Any frying up there is a violation.
- No fire extinguisher visible through binoculars. The report says never use water on it.
- Something steaming near the container at noon. The Board did not see what it was. The Board did not need to.

The report also states that a frozen turkey lowered into hot oil can explode. The Board asks: explode facing which direction? The container faces Mt. Blanca. Who benefits?

The Board's own holiday meal was a survival food bucket from the old InfoWars store, which has never once exploded.

A new sign went up beside the others: "NO FRYING WITHIN 10 FT. COSTILLA COUNTY IS ZONED." The Board is watching from the porch.
